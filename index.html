Same Metric, Opposite Verdicts: How Simplification Benchmarks Confound the Evaluation of Metrics, and How to Evaluate Around It

Abstract

Text-simplification metrics are validated by correlating them with human ratings of meaning preservation and simplicity. We show that the outcome of this validation depends on the benchmark as much as on the metric. A meta-analysis of nine public benchmarks finds that the human correlation between meaning and simplicity ranges from −0.37 to +0.74 (I² = 0.99), so a metric that tracks only meaning appears to reward simplicity on one benchmark and to penalise it on another. We describe each metric by its fingerprint, the weights it puts on meaning and on simplicity once that correlation is removed. Fingerprints need only three correlations, are stable across benchmarks for most metrics, and rank metrics far more consistently than raw correlations (Kendall τ gain 0.28). Output length is a second confound, and how strongly it is tied to simplicity is itself a property of the benchmark. A factorial contrast set, in which meaning, simplicity and length vary independently, separates the three: in a pilot, readability formulas and learned simplicity metrics track simplicity cleanly, length ratios carry none of the designed simplicity, and only two metrics track meaning. Two open LLM judges are the strongest meaning measures we tested; for simplicity they match the best metric only under a carefully worded prompt, and, like every automatic measure we tested, they treat shorter as simpler. We turn these results into a checklist for evaluating simplification metrics.

1Introduction

A simplification metric is usually trusted because its scores correlate with human ratings. Most benchmarks rate two things, meaning preservation (M) and simplicity (S), and a metric is said to "measure simplicity" when it correlates with S. That verdict is not a property of the metric alone. BERTScore correlates with simplicity at r = +0.56 on Simplicity-DA and at r = −0.22 on Structural Simplicity. The metric did not change; the benchmark did.

The reason is that benchmarks differ in how their own human ratings of meaning and simplicity relate. On some, annotators who see a faithful output also tend to find it simple; on others, the simpler outputs are the ones that lost content. Call this correlation ρ = r(M, S). A metric that tracks only meaning inherits a simplicity correlation of about ρ · r(X, M): positive where ρ is positive and negative where it is negative. Raw validation correlations therefore mix what a metric measures with what the benchmark's annotators did (). On top of this, simplicity on natural data is tangled with length, because simplifying usually means shortening.

This paper measures the confound, gives a way to remove it, and turns the result into practice. We make five contributions:

  1. The confound, measured. The first meta-analysis of ρ across simplification benchmarks: on all nine public English benchmarks that rate meaning and simplicity separately, it ranges from −0.37 to +0.74, and that range explains where benchmarks disagree about metrics ().
  2. Fingerprints. A closed-form description of a metric by its weights on meaning and simplicity given ρ, with a feasibility bound, a correction for rating noise, and ranking and transfer tests (). Fingerprints are stable for 82% of the metrics and make metric rankings agree across benchmarks ().
  3. Length, separated. Partial correlations and equal-length pairs on natural data, and a factorial contrast set in which meaning, simplicity and length are crossed by design and rated by people (, ).
  4. LLM judges through the same lens. Two open judges against every metric, the effect of how a judge is scored, its length shortcut, and its sensitivity to the prompt (–).
  5. A how-to. A checklist for evaluating simplification metrics, each step tied to the evidence for it, with a fingerprint calculator ().

The analyses of the nine benchmarks were pre-registered; we report every pre-registered test, including those that failed ().

2Related Work

Correlated criteria in meta-evaluation. Ernst et al. (2023) show on SummEval that metrics designed for one quality criterion appear to correlate with others because the human criteria themselves correlate, and remove the effect by stratifying. Shayegh et al. (2025) find that machine-translation meta-evaluation favours adequacy metrics over fluency metrics partly because of the systems it contains. Pagnoni et al. (2021) use partial correlations for factuality metrics, and Xiao et al. (2023) bring measurement theory to metric evaluation. That metric conclusions change from dataset to dataset is documented by Bhandari et al. (2020) and Dai et al. (2024), without an explanation through the correlation between human criteria. We model the effect in closed form, correct it for rating noise, and test its predictions across benchmarks. The bound on a correlation given two others (Stanley & Wang 1969; Glass & Collins 1970; Olkin 1981) and the correction for attenuation (Spearman 1904) are classical; we use them as such.

Evaluating simplification. SARI (Xu et al. 2016) and BLEU, whose use for simplification Sulem et al. (2018) criticise, compare outputs with references; LENS, LENS-SALSA, MeaningBERT and SIMPLE are learned; readability formulas and length ratios serve as proxies. Alva-Manchego et al. (2021) find that how well metrics agree with human judgments depends on the outputs being judged. SLE (Cripwell et al. 2023) observes that metrics conflate simplicity with meaning and reports very different simplicity–adequacy correlations on two benchmarks. We measure that correlation on every public benchmark and show what it does to every metric.

Length. Napoles et al. (2011) ask for comparisons of compression systems at equal compression, and Martin et al. (2018) find simplicity on QATS best predicted by length features. We measure, per benchmark and per metric, how much of a simplicity correlation survives once length is held fixed.

Contrast sets and LLM judges. Contrast sets (Gardner et al. 2020), behavioural tests (CheckList) and perturbation suites for metrics (DEMETR; He et al. 2023; APPLS) mostly vary one factor at a time and are rarely rated by people; ours crosses three factors and is human-rated. LLM judges are commonly scored by the expected rating over the output digits (G-Eval). Their verbosity bias, a preference for longer answers, is established in pairwise preference (Zheng et al. 2023; Dubois et al. 2024); for simplicity we find the opposite shortcut.

3Method

3.1The confound

Let X be a metric's score on the outputs of a benchmark, and M and S the mean human meaning and simplicity ratings of the same outputs, with ρ = r(M, S). Think of the metric's score as a recipe with two ingredients: some amount of "does the output keep the meaning of the source?" and some amount of "is it simpler than the source?". A metric that cares only about meaning still correlates with simplicity on a benchmark where faithful outputs also happen to be the simple ones: it picks simplicity up through meaning. Each raw correlation is the sum of a direct path, which belongs to the metric, and an indirect path through the other rating, whose size is set by the benchmark's ρ:

r(X, S) = βS + ρ · βM   and   r(X, M) = βM + ρ · βS
raw correlation = direct path + path through the other rating

The usual validation reports the left-hand sides. Their indirect parts change sign with ρ, and ρ is a property of the benchmark.

3.2Fingerprints

The fingerprint of a metric on a benchmark keeps the direct paths and removes the indirect ones. It is the pair of standardised coefficients of a regression of X on M and S:

  • βM, the weight on meaning: how much the score moves with human meaning when simplicity is held fixed;
  • βS, the weight on simplicity: how much it moves with human simplicity when meaning is held fixed.

It needs only the three correlations a standard metric evaluation already reports or can report, ρ, rM = r(X, M) and rS = r(X, S). Solving the two path equations gives

βM = (rM − ρ rS) / (1 − ρ²)     βS = (rS − ρ rM) / (1 − ρ²)
in matrix form β = Σ−1c, with Σ = [[1, ρ], [ρ, 1]] and c = (rM, rS)

Alignment. R² = βM rM + βS rS is the share of the metric's variance that the two ratings explain together, and R is the correlation between the metric and the best linear mix of the two. When R is small the metric barely relates to either rating and the direction of its fingerprint means little; we interpret a fingerprint only where R ≥ 0.20. Because only correlations enter, fingerprints are comparable across benchmarks with different rating scales.

Feasibility bound. The correlation matrix of X, M and S must be positive semi-definite, which holds exactly when R² ≤ 1. This confines (rM, rS) to an ellipse whose tilt is set by ρ alone: where ρ is negative, no metric can correlate strongly with both meaning and simplicity. The trade-off is then built into the benchmark, not into the metric.

Rating noise. Noisy ratings shrink every correlation. Where a benchmark releases per-rater scores we estimate split-half reliabilities RM and RS and compute a disattenuated fingerprint from ρ* = ρ / √(RMRS) and c* = c / √R.

takes the computation apart for any metric and benchmark.

Three worked examples. BERTScore, a meaning metric that borrows simplicity. On Simplicity-DA (ρ = 0.74) BERTScore correlates +0.56 with human simplicity, which would make it a respectable simplicity metric. Split into paths, +0.55 of that arrives through meaning (0.74 × βM = 0.74) and only +0.01 is direct. On Structural Simplicity (ρ = −0.37) the same meaning path contributes −0.30, and the raw correlation becomes −0.22. Its weight on simplicity stays near zero on both (+0.01 and +0.08). SIMPLE-large, the mirror case. βS = +0.58 on both benchmarks, with a small βM (+0.16, +0.15); its correlation with meaning is the borrowed one: +0.59 on Simplicity-DA, −0.07 on Structural Simplicity. Compression ratio, where the paths cancel. On Simplicity-DA compression ratio correlates only +0.08 with simplicity and would be dismissed as unrelated to it. Its fingerprint says the opposite: βS = −0.56 (longer outputs are less simple) and βM = +0.87 (longer outputs keep more content); the meaning path adds 0.74 × 0.87 = +0.64, which almost exactly cancels the direct −0.56. A raw correlation near zero can hide two strong, opposite effects.

Prediction. Run forwards, the path equations predict the raw correlations a metric with a given fingerprint would show on any benchmark, from that benchmark's ρ alone (). This gives two tests. Transfer: predict a metric's correlations on a held-out benchmark from its mean fingerprint on the others, and compare with the naive prediction, the mean raw correlations on the others. Ranking consistency: for every pair of benchmarks, Kendall τb between the two metric rankings, by raw rS and by βS.

What a fingerprint is not. It is not causal: it describes what the score co-varies with in one benchmark's outputs, holding the other rating fixed, and a different mix of systems can shift it, which is why we test stability rather than assume it. It is not defined when the two ratings coincide: uncertainty grows by 1/(1 − ρ²), at most 2.2 on our benchmarks (|ρ| ≤ 0.74). And it is only as good as its dimensions: anything else the metric tracks is either unexplained (lower R) or, if it correlates with M or S, attributed to them. Length is the obvious candidate, which is why we add it as a third dimension.

3.3Length

We take length L as the compression ratio (output tokens / source tokens). Three views separate a metric's simplicity signal from length: the partial correlation r(X, S | L), which says how much of the metric's simplicity correlation survives once length is held fixed; a three-way fingerprint on (M, S, L), with the same formula and a 3 × 3 Σ; and accuracy on equal-length pairs, two outputs of the same source with |ΔL| ≤ 0.05 and clearly different human simplicity, where length cannot help.

3.4A factorial contrast set

On natural data, meaning, simplicity and length move together, so no analysis of natural data alone can say what a metric would do if they did not. We therefore build outputs that land in the cells of a 2 × 3 × 3 design: meaning kept or lost × simpler, same or harder than the source × shorter, same length or longer. With equal cell counts the three factors are uncorrelated, so a metric's weights on meaning, simplicity and length are separately identifiable. Each output is generated for a cell, checked by a human validator for whether it realises its cell, and rated by annotators. No metric or LLM ever selects items, since the set is meant to test exactly those scorers. Because each item carries its designed labels, every analysis can also be run against the design instead of the ratings, which needs no annotator.

3.5LLM judges as metrics

An LLM judge is prompted with the source and the output and asked one question per dimension, using the annotators' own definitions and scales; its score is the expected rating over the probabilities of the rating digits (G-Eval). We then treat each judge as one more metric: same items, same correlations, same fingerprints, same length tests. Two further questions are specific to judges. Scoring: a judge that reasons before answering puts almost all probability on one digit, so its expected score is its answered digit, and comparing it with a judge that answers directly compares two scoring rules as well as two models. Prompting: to see how much a judge's scores depend on the wording of its prompt, we ask the same two questions under four more prompts that ask for the judgment in very different ways (; full texts in ). Every prompt is answered with one digit from 1 to 5, scored the same way and mapped onto the main prompt's scales, so only the wording changes.

4Experimental Setup

4.1Benchmarks

We use every public English simplification benchmark that rates meaning and simplicity separately: nine studies, from 84 to 6,163 rated outputs, built between 2016 and 2024 with different systems, scales and rater pools (). One row is one study: a re-rating of the same outputs by other raters or with other questions counts as a separate study; copied ratings do not. Human scores are means over raters, standardised within each benchmark, and ρ is the item-level correlation between the mean meaning and simplicity ratings.

4.2Metrics

Nineteen metrics in three groups: similarity to references or to the source (BLEU, SARI, iBLEU, BERTScore; BLEU and BERTScore against the source; SAMSA); learned metrics (LENS, LENS-SALSA, MeaningBERT, SIMPLE small, base and large); and length and readability proxies (compression ratio; output-versus-source gains in sentence length, word length, FKGL, FRE and SLE). A metric enters every comparison in its useful direction (for example "−BLEU (src)" for simplicity). Our scores reproduce all 19 recomputable correlations published in the SIMPLE paper within 0.01.

4.3Contrast set

Sources are hard-to-read sentences (20–45 tokens, FKGL ≥ 10) published in 2025–26 on Wikipedia, Wikinews, MedlinePlus and gov.uk, excluding near-duplicates of benchmark and metric-training data. Two open models generate the outputs (Gemma 3 27B and Mistral Small 3.2 24B), from families other than the LLM judges. Trained annotators rate meaning (1–5), simplicity relative to the source (−2…+2) and fluency (1–5). The full set has 150 sources and about 1,050 items rated by three annotators; the results below come from its pilot, 20 sources and 199 items (194 designed items and 5 attention checks).

Exploratory: pilot, one annotator
The pilot is rated by one trained annotator so far. Agreement (Krippendorff α) needs a second and third annotator, and single-rater ratings are noisier than the three-rater means the full set will use. Contrast-set numbers in this draft are a first look at the design, not results.

4.4LLM judges

Qwen3.8-27B (dense, GGUF Q8_0, thinking off; it answers directly) and gpt-oss-120b (mixture of experts, native MXFP4, low reasoning effort; it reasons before answering), served locally with llama.cpp at temperature 0. Scores are expected values over the top-10 digit log-probabilities; gpt-oss puts probability ≈ 1 on one digit, so its expected score is its answered digit. The natural sample has at most 2,000 items per benchmark, drawn by whole sources (5,994 items), plus the contrast pilot. The prompt study uses 739 items, the 199 pilot items and 60 items per natural benchmark spread over its sources, each answered under all five prompts by both judges (about 5,900 calls per judge).

4.5Statistics and pre-registration

Item-level Pearson correlations; 95% percentile cluster bootstrap over source sentences (B = 10,000, seed 13; B = 2,000 for differences between a judge and a metric); paired differences use the same bootstrap replicates. ρ is pooled with a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects meta-analysis on Fisher z. Hypotheses H1–H8 and their thresholds were frozen on 13 November 2026, before the analyses, and a script checks their hash; Holm correction applies within the confirmatory family {H1, H2, H3, H8}. Analyses added later are marked exploratory. Details are in , hypotheses and verdicts in .

5Results

5.1Benchmarks disagree about how meaning and simplicity relate

The nine benchmarks do not agree on how their own two ratings relate ().

Four benchmarks have ρ ≥ 0.32, four are near zero, and one is clearly negative; the heterogeneity is extreme (Q = 971 on 8 df, I² = 0.99).

The spread is the confound of made concrete. A metric that tracks only meaning shows a simplicity correlation of 0.74 times its weight on meaning on Simplicity-DA and of −0.37 times it on Structural Simplicity (). The spread also limits what any metric can achieve: places every metric inside the region its benchmark allows, and where ρ is negative that region excludes any metric that correlates strongly with both ratings.

5.2Fingerprints are stable and make rankings agree

Five profiles cover almost every metric we tested (; places them all). They answer the question a practitioner actually has: "does this metric measure simplicity?" becomes "how much of it is simplicity, how much is meaning, and with which sign?". A metric in the bottom two rows can look like a good simplicity metric or a good meaning metric on one benchmark, depending on ρ, while rewarding the wrong thing on the other dimension.

For 14 of 17 aligned metrics (82%) the SD of βS across benchmarks is at most 0.15 (pre-registered bar: 70%); the median SD falls from 0.225 for the raw correlation to 0.138 for the fingerprint.

The three exceptions (SARI, iBLEU and BERTScore against the source) are all similarity metrics; among reference-free metrics, 11 of 12 fingerprints are stable. Stability matters in practice because it makes metric rankings agree across benchmarks ().

The stronger, pre-registered claim, that fingerprints predict a metric's correlations on an unseen benchmark 40% better than the naive baseline, does not hold. The leave-one-benchmark-out error falls by 24% (0.153 against 0.200), and the fingerprint wins for 19 of 19 metrics. The gain is concentrated where it should be: on benchmarks whose ρ is far from the others' ().

5.3Length is part of the confound

How strongly simplicity and length are linked is itself a benchmark property, from |r| = 0.05 on ASSET to 0.71 on Structural Simplicity (a). If human simplicity ratings follow length, a metric can look like a simplicity metric by rewarding brevity.

Compression ratio and sentence-length gain lose their whole simplicity signal once length is controlled and sit at chance on equal-length pairs. FKGL, FRE and ΔSLE keep 70–80% of theirs, and SIMPLE keeps 93–96%.

The pre-registered expectation was stronger: that proxies as a group lose ≥ 60% (they lose 33%) and fall to chance on equal-length pairs (the readability formulas reach 0.57–0.60). Readability formulas therefore carry simplicity signal beyond length, and length contaminates learned metrics far less than expected. What natural data cannot do is settle the question where it matters most: only on Structural Simplicity are simplicity and length strongly coupled (|r| = 0.71), and there a three-way fingerprint on (M, S, L) is precise, so the inseparability hypothesis H4 fails. Separating simplicity from length cleanly requires data in which they are uncorrelated by design.

5.4The contrast set separates what natural data cannot

Did the design survive? Before annotation, the designed factors and the measured length are uncorrelated on the valid items (all |r| ≤ 0.04). After annotation, the ratings mostly follow the design: the achieved correlation between human meaning and human simplicity is 0.04, and between meaning and length −0.12. Simplicity and length are still weakly linked in the ratings (r = −0.25 against a 0.20 target): longer outputs are rated a little harder even when the design keeps their difficulty fixed ().

Two weak spots stand out. In the "meaning lost" cells the annotator often still gave high meaning scores: generated losses that drop a minor detail read as harmless, and lenient raters miss them (cell validity for meaning 0.64 against a 0.85 target). And some cells are intrinsically hard to generate: lengthening a sentence by explaining it reads as simpler even when the model is told to keep the difficulty, itself a sign of how entangled simplicity and length are.

Metrics on the contrast set. The contrast set changes what the metrics appear to measure ().

Readability formulas and learned simplicity metrics correlate 0.62–0.74 with human simplicity, far above their natural-data average, with essentially no meaning signal. Only MeaningBERT (0.51) and BERTScore against the source (0.37) track meaning; BLEU against the source, often used as a meaning proxy, does not (0.10).

Both shifts are what the confound predicts. On natural benchmarks a readability formula's simplicity correlation is diluted wherever ρ works against it, and similarity to the source looks like a meaning measure partly because faithful outputs are also the ones that copy the source. With meaning and simplicity uncorrelated by design (ρ = 0.09 in these ratings), neither distortion is there. Length ratios behave as designed against the designed simplicity labels (|r| ≤ 0.04) but keep |r| ≈ 0.25 against this annotator's ratings, the size of the residual simplicity–length link in the ratings themselves. All three pre-registered H6 predictions fail in this pilot, in an informative direction: readability formulas do not collapse (consistent with ), and the learned metrics' fingerprints move away from their natural-data values. Both need confirming on the full set.

For meaning, both judges are the best or near-best measure: Qwen3.8-27B is first or tied with the best metric on 8 of 9 benchmarks and significantly better on 5; gpt-oss-120b on 7 and 3. On the contrast set both are far ahead (0.77 and 0.70 against MeaningBERT's 0.51). 5.6Every automatic simplicity measure treats shorter as simpler

We test whether a judge's simplicity score depends on length beyond what the human ratings explain: partial r(judge S, L | human M, human S), with the same test on two metrics for comparison. On the contrast set we can also control for the design instead of the ratings, which lets us run the test on the human annotator too.

The pre-registered bias finding (|partial r| ≥ 0.15 with the CI excluding 0, for at least one judge) holds on natural data for both judges: Qwen3.8-27B on three benchmarks (Wu & Arase −0.52, QATS 2016 −0.44, Scialom (human) −0.30), gpt-oss-120b on six (Wu & Arase −0.47, Scialom (human) −0.40, QATS 2016 −0.35, SimpDA 2022 −0.28, Simplicity-DA −0.20, SimpLikert 2022 −0.20). On the contrast-set pilot it is −0.38 and −0.50.

This is not the familiar verbosity bias of pairwise preference judges, which favour longer answers: for simplicity the judges lean the other way, and on the natural benchmarks more than a readability formula does (Wu & Arase: −0.52 and −0.47 against −0.18 for FKGL). Noisy human ratings under-control for true simplicity, so part of a natural-data partial correlation can be real simplicity. The contrast set gives the cleaner test, because length is uncorrelated with the design, and there the picture is broader (, top row): controlling for the designed factors, every simplicity measure treats shorter as simpler more than the human annotator does (−0.32): gpt-oss most (−0.66), then Qwen (−0.61), but FKGL (−0.57) and SIMPLE-large (−0.54) nearly as much. The shortcut is not specific to LLM judges; they are simply not free of it.

5.7A judge's simplicity score depends on its prompt

Everything above uses the main prompt j1. Under the four other prompts of (exploratory, not pre-registered), meaning and simplicity behave very differently ().

For meaning the prompt hardly matters. Averaged over the nine benchmarks the five prompts give 0.72–0.79 (Qwen3.8-27B) and 0.64–0.71 (gpt-oss-120b), and the judges' standing against the metrics does not change: Qwen is significantly better than the best metric on four to six of the ten data sets under every prompt, and worse on none. For simplicity the prompt matters a great deal. j1 is the best of the five for both judges (0.60 and 0.57); no other prompt beats it significantly on any data set, and 11 (gpt-oss) and 12 (Qwen) of the 40 prompt × data-set comparisons are significantly worse. Without a definition (bare), simplicity validity falls to 0.27 and 0.26 on average, and to between −0.02 and 0.22 on ASSET and Scialom (system), where j1 reaches 0.73–0.76. With j1 the judges tie the best metric on every data set (Qwen beats it on the contrast pilot); under the other prompts they fall below it on one to seven of the ten.

A judge under two prompts agrees with itself on meaning (0.91 Qwen, 0.81 gpt-oss) but much less on simplicity (0.56 and 0.48, and 0.66 and 0.61 with bare left out). The two judges given the same j1 prompt agree on simplicity at 0.74 (): rewording the prompt moves a judge about as much as replacing the model. What moves is what the simplicity score measures. On the natural benchmarks its weight on human simplicity is 0.59 and 0.57 under j1, and 0.31–0.56 under the other prompts. With explain it also picks up meaning (βM 0.23 and 0.20, against 0.03 and 0.04 under j1), and gpt-oss's bare score rewards losing meaning (βM −0.28). The meaning score's fingerprint stays where it was (βM 0.67–0.80 under every prompt). On the contrast pilot, against the design, j1's simplicity score follows designed simplicity with almost no meaning in it (βS 0.77 and 0.67, βM 0.07 and 0.04); bare weakens it (0.56 and 0.51), and the reader framing makes Qwen's meaning score partly a simplicity score (βS 0.26).

The length shortcut of is not a product of j1's wording. On the contrast pilot, controlling for the design, every prompt treats shorter as simpler, for both judges (partial r −0.47 to −0.74). On the natural benchmarks the bare prompt, which lacks j1's note that simplicity "is not about length", makes the bias much stronger (Qwen: −0.25 to −0.78, meeting the pre-registered bias criterion on eight of the nine benchmarks); whether the note itself is what helps would need a prompt that differs from j1 by the note alone.

A judge's meaning score is a property of the model; its simplicity score is a property of the model and its prompt. The simplicity results of belong to one carefully worded prompt, the best of the five we tried, and are a best case.

The prompt study is exploratory: with 60 items per natural benchmark the intervals are wide, and the 40 comparisons per judge and dimension are not corrected for multiplicity. Two prompts change more than the wording (bare drops the definition; reader asks about the rewrite's own simplicity rather than its gain over the original); the conclusions hold with bare left out.

6How to Evaluate a Simplification Metric

The results reduce to a short procedure. Each step needs at most a few correlations that a standard evaluation already computes, and each points to the evidence for it.

  1. Report the benchmark's ρ next to every metric–human correlation. Without it a simplicity correlation cannot be read: a metric that tracks only meaning shows a simplicity correlation of about ρ · r(X, M), and ρ ranges from −0.37 to +0.74 across the public benchmarks ().
  2. Report the fingerprint (βM, βS) and R, not only the raw correlations, and read its direction only where R ≥ 0.20. Three correlations suffice ().
  3. Compare metrics across benchmarks by fingerprint. Rankings by βS agree across benchmarks far more than rankings by raw correlation, most of all between benchmarks with different ρ ().
  4. Check length. Report the benchmark's r(S, L) and the metric's simplicity correlation with length partialled out; where |r(S, L)| ≥ 0.3, add the three-way fingerprint or accuracy on equal-length pairs ().
  5. Test on data where the factors are crossed. A factorial contrast set works as a unit test: a simplicity metric should follow designed simplicity and not meaning or length, a meaning metric the reverse ().
  6. Run the length test on every simplicity measure, judges included. Every automatic measure we tested treats shorter as simpler beyond what the annotator does ().
  7. For an LLM judge, report the prompt, its definition of simplicity and the scoring rule, and results under more than one prompt; compare judges under the same scoring rule (, ).

7Discussion

Reading published metric comparisons. A comparison of simplification metrics on one benchmark is a comparison under that benchmark's ρ. Where ρ is high, meaning metrics look like simplicity metrics; where it is negative, the same metrics appear to penalise simplicity. A conclusion drawn on one benchmark needs its ρ to be read, and the conclusions that carry over are those that hold across benchmarks with different ρ.

What the contrast set adds. On natural data, a metric's meaning signal and its copying of the source come together. When meaning, simplicity and length are crossed by design, most metrics that looked like meaning metrics are not, and the metrics that look like simplicity metrics mostly are. The contrast set is the evidence natural benchmarks cannot provide, and its design labels allow every test to run without annotators.

LLM judges. For meaning, open LLM judges are now the strongest automatic measures we tested, and a 27B judge is at least as good as a 120B one. For simplicity they are as good as the best metric, but only with a carefully worded prompt, and they share the metrics' length shortcut. Any comparison of judges is also a comparison of their scoring rules and prompts.

8Conclusion

Validation correlations for simplification metrics mix what a metric measures with how a benchmark's annotators relate meaning and simplicity, and that relation ranges from strongly positive to negative across the public benchmarks. Fingerprints remove the mix with three correlations and make metric rankings agree across benchmarks; a length check and a factorial contrast set separate meaning, simplicity and length. Through the same lens, LLM judges are the best meaning measures available and, for simplicity, as good as their prompt. The checklist of makes these checks routine.

Limitations

English only. The nine benchmarks are of very uneven size (84 to 6,163 rated outputs), and four of them have ρ near zero. Length is measured as the compression ratio. The fingerprint is linear and item-level, and system-level conclusions may differ. The contrast-set items are generated by two open models, so their realism has to be shown, and the results in this draft come from a one-annotator pilot. The judges are two open models from two families; closed models may behave differently. The prompt study is exploratory and small. One pre-registered criterion (transfer, H2) was not met.

The contrast set is built from public texts published in 2025–26; its annotation was approved by an ethics review, and annotators were trained before rating [compensation details to add]. BStatistical details

Correlations and intervals. Item-level Pearson correlations; 95% percentile cluster bootstrap over source sentences (B = 10,000, seed 13; B = 2,000 for judge-versus-metric differences and the prompt study). Meta-analysis. DerSimonian–Laird random-effects pooling of ρ on Fisher z; Q, I² and τ². Multiplicity. Holm correction within the confirmatory family {H1, H2, H3, H8}. Reliability. Split-half per dimension (random rater halves, Spearman–Brown, 200 splits); ρ* = ρ / √(RMRS) and c* = c / √R. Ranking consistency. Kendall τb between the metric rankings of two benchmarks, by raw rS and by βS. Length. L = compression ratio; partial r(X, S | L); equal-length pairs = two outputs of one source with |ΔL| ≤ 0.05 and clearly different human simplicity. Judges. Expected score over the top-10 digit log-probabilities; for the prompt study, prompts with a reversed scale are flipped back and the reader prompt's absolute simplicity is mapped onto −2…+2.

Proposals

Suggestions for maximising the paper's chances at ARR, January 2027; each says what it costs.

Proposal · framing (adopted in this draft)
  • Lead with the confound and a recommendation, not with the method. The flip figure opens the paper and gives the take-home; the pre-registered H2 miss is stated plainly as "transfer helps where benchmarks differ". Cost: writing only.
Proposal · analyses that pre-empt reviewer questions
  • Rater-free contrast analyses as co-primary. Every contrast-set result also against the validated design labels (already implemented). It protects the contrast-set story against annotator noise and the annotation budget. Cost: none; reporting only.
  • Score every judge the same way. A reasoning judge gives one digit; sample it several times (e.g. five samples at temperature 0.7) and average, so its score is graded like a direct judge's, and report both judges with both scoring rules. It removes the resolution confound from the judge comparison before a reviewer finds it. Cost: about 5× the gpt-oss calls on the contrast pilot and one natural benchmark, ~1 GPU-day on the shared machine.
  • Prompt robustness as a confirmed result. Run items 61–300 of each benchmark (new items: the sampling order is fixed) with predictions written down before the run, and add two controlled prompts: j1 reworded with the same content, and j1 without its length note. The first separates wording from definition, which answers the obvious objection to the bare prompt; the second shows what the length note does. Cost: about 18 h (Qwen) and 16 h (gpt-oss) of judge time on the shared machine, two to three days.
  • A length-controlled judge. j1 already says that simplicity "is not about length", and the bias is there anyway, and stronger without that note. The j1-without-the-note prompt above measures what the note does; a stronger instruction, with its fingerprint and partial r, would turn the bias finding into a usable recommendation. Cost: about 3 GPU-hours on the contrast pilot.
  • One closed API judge for external validity on the contrast set and a 2,000-item natural subsample. Cost: small; about 10k calls.
  • System-level fingerprints in an appendix, for the benchmarks with at least six systems, because many metric papers report system-level correlations. Cost: one analysis on existing scores.
Proposal · resources
  • Fingerprint cards: one row per metric and judge (βM, βS, R, stability, length retention, contrast-set r), as the paper's practical table. Cost: assembled from existing results.
