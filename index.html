Same Metric, Opposite Verdicts: How Simplification Benchmarks Confound the Evaluation of Metrics

Abstract

Text-simplification metrics are validated by correlating them with human ratings of meaning preservation and simplicity. We show that the outcome of this validation depends on the benchmark. Across nine public benchmarks the human correlation between meaning and simplicity ranges from −0.37 to +0.74 (I² = 0.99), so a metric that only tracks meaning appears to reward simplicity on one benchmark and to penalise it on another. We describe each metric by its fingerprint, the weights it puts on meaning and on simplicity once their correlation is removed. Fingerprints are stable for most metrics and rank metrics far more consistently across benchmarks than raw correlations (Kendall τ gain 0.28). On natural data, simplicity is also entangled with output length, to a degree that differs from benchmark to benchmark. A factorial contrast set, in which meaning, simplicity and length vary independently, pulls the two apart: in a pilot, readability formulas and learned simplicity metrics track simplicity more cleanly than on any natural benchmark, length ratios carry none of the designed simplicity, and only two metrics track meaning at all. Two open LLM judges (Qwen3.8-27B and gpt-oss-120b) are the best meaning measures on most benchmarks and on the contrast set, are on par with the best metric for simplicity, and reward shorter outputs more than human raters do; the larger judge is not the better one, partly because of how its answers can be scored. Their simplicity results, unlike their meaning results, depend on the prompt: across five prompts their meaning validity barely moves, while their simplicity validity ranges from 0.26 to 0.60 (exploratory).

1Introduction

A simplification metric is usually trusted because its scores correlate with human ratings. Most benchmarks rate two things, meaning preservation (M) and simplicity (S), and a metric is said to "measure simplicity" when it correlates with S. That verdict is not a property of the metric alone. BERTScore correlates with simplicity at r = +0.53 on Simplicity-DA and at r = −0.31 on Structural Simplicity. The metric did not change; the benchmark did.

The reason is that benchmarks differ in how their own human ratings of meaning and simplicity relate. On some, annotators who see a faithful output also tend to find it simple; on others, simpler outputs are the ones that lost content. Call this correlation ρ = r(M, S). A metric that tracks only meaning inherits a simplicity correlation of about ρ · r(X, M): positive where ρ is positive and negative where it is negative. Raw correlations therefore mix what a metric measures with what the benchmark's annotators did. On top of this, simplicity on natural data is tangled with length, because simplifying usually means shortening.

This paper makes four contributions:

  1. The confound, measured. The first meta-analysis of ρ across simplification benchmarks (nine, pre-registered): it varies from −0.37 to +0.74, and that variation explains where benchmarks disagree about metrics (§2).
  2. Fingerprints. A closed-form description of a metric by its weights on M and S given ρ, with a feasibility bound, a reliability correction and a transfer test. Fingerprints are stable for 82% of metrics and make metric rankings agree across benchmarks (§3).
  3. Length, separated. Partial correlations and matched-length pairs on natural data (§4), and a factorial contrast set in which meaning, simplicity and length are crossed by design and rated by humans (§5).
  4. LLM judges. How LLM judges compare with every metric on the benchmarks and on the contrast set, and whether they reward shorter outputs beyond what humans do (§6).

2Benchmarks disagree about how meaning and simplicity relate

We use every public English benchmark that rates meaning and simplicity separately: nine studies, from 84 to 6,163 rated outputs, built between 2016 and 2024 with different systems, scales and rater pools (Appendix A). For each, ρ is the item-level correlation between the mean human meaning and simplicity ratings.

Four benchmarks have ρ ≥ 0.32, four are near zero, and one is clearly negative; the heterogeneity is extreme (Q = 971 on 8 df, I² = 0.99).

What this does to a metric is easiest to see by putting two benchmarks side by side. Pick any two below. With Simplicity-DA (ρ = 0.74) and Structural Simplicity (ρ = −0.37), the similarity metrics (BLEU, BERTScore, SARI) swap the sign of their simplicity correlation, while their fingerprint weight on simplicity stays near zero on both.

The same arithmetic also limits what any metric can achieve on a benchmark. The joint correlation matrix of a metric X and the two human ratings must be positive semi-definite, which confines the pair (r(X, M), r(X, S)) to an ellipse whose tilt is set by ρ alone. Where ρ is negative, no metric can correlate strongly with both meaning and simplicity: the trade-off is built into the benchmark, not into the metric.

3Fingerprints: what a metric responds to, once ρ is removed

3.1The intuition

Think of a metric's score as a recipe with two ingredients: some amount of "does the output keep the meaning of the source?" and some amount of "is it simpler than the source?". The fingerprint is that recipe, written as two numbers:

  • βM, the weight on meaning: how much the metric's score moves with human meaning ratings when simplicity is held fixed;
  • βS, the weight on simplicity: how much it moves with human simplicity ratings when meaning is held fixed.

The usual validation correlation does not measure this recipe. A metric that cares only about meaning will still correlate with simplicity on a benchmark where the faithful outputs also happen to be the simple ones: it picks simplicity up through meaning. Each raw correlation is the sum of a direct path, which belongs to the metric, and an indirect path through the other rating, whose size is set by the benchmark's ρ:

r(X, S) = βS + ρ · βM   and   r(X, M) = βM + ρ · βS
raw correlation = direct path + path through the other rating

The fingerprint keeps the direct paths and removes the indirect ones. Pick a metric and a benchmark in Figure 4 to see the two paths with real numbers.

Three examples.

BERTScore, a meaning metric that borrows simplicity. On Simplicity-DA (ρ = 0.74) BERTScore correlates +0.56 with human simplicity, which would make it a respectable simplicity metric. Split into paths, +0.55 of that arrives through meaning (0.74 × βM = 0.74) and only +0.01 is direct. On Structural Simplicity (ρ = −0.37) the same meaning path contributes −0.30, and the raw correlation becomes −0.22. Its weight on simplicity stays near zero on both (+0.01 and +0.08): BERTScore measures meaning, and its "simplicity correlation" is borrowed from the benchmark.

SIMPLE-large, the mirror case. βS = +0.58 on both benchmarks, with a small βM (+0.16, +0.15). Its correlation with meaning is the borrowed one: +0.59 on Simplicity-DA, −0.07 on Structural Simplicity.

Compression ratio, where the paths cancel. On Simplicity-DA, compression ratio correlates only +0.08 with simplicity and would be dismissed as unrelated to it. Its fingerprint says the opposite: βS = −0.56 (longer outputs are less simple) and βM = +0.87 (longer outputs keep more content). The meaning path adds 0.74 × 0.87 = +0.64, which almost exactly cancels the direct −0.56. On Structural Simplicity both paths point the same way and the raw correlation is −0.71. A raw correlation near zero can hide two strong, opposite effects.

3.2How it is computed

Within one benchmark, the fingerprint is the pair of standardised coefficients of a regression of the metric's score X on the mean human meaning (M) and simplicity (S) ratings. It needs only three correlations, all of which a standard metric evaluation already reports or can report:

  1. ρ = r(M, S), the correlation between the two human ratings on that benchmark;
  2. rM = r(X, M) and rS = r(X, S), the metric's usual validation correlations;
  3. solve the two path equations of §3.1 for the weights:
βM = (rM − ρ rS) / (1 − ρ²)     βS = (rS − ρ rM) / (1 − ρ²)
in matrix form β = Σ−1c, with Σ = [[1, ρ], [ρ, 1]] and c = (rM, rS)

A third number says how much the fingerprint can be trusted. R² = βM rM + βS rS is the share of the metric's variance that the two human ratings explain together, and R is the correlation between the metric and the best linear mix of the two ratings. When R is small the metric barely relates to either rating and the direction of its fingerprint means little; we interpret a fingerprint only where R ≥ 0.20 (hollow marks elsewhere). Confidence intervals come from a cluster bootstrap over source sentences. Because only correlations enter, fingerprints are comparable across benchmarks with different rating scales (0–100 sliders, 1–5 Likert, good/ok/bad).

3.5What a fingerprint is not

  • Not causal. It describes what the score co-varies with in one benchmark's outputs, holding the other rating fixed. A different mix of systems can shift it, which is why stability is tested below rather than assumed.
  • Not defined when the ratings coincide. The division by 1 − ρ² means that as |ρ| approaches 1 the two ratings carry the same information and the weights cannot be told apart; uncertainty grows by 1/(1 − ρ²). On our benchmarks (|ρ| ≤ 0.74) that factor is at most 2.2.
  • Only as good as its two dimensions. Anything else the metric tracks (fluency, length) is either unexplained (lower R) or, if it correlates with M or S, attributed to them. §4 adds length as a third dimension with the same formula and a 3 × 3 Σ.
  • Attenuated by noisy ratings. Rater noise shrinks every correlation; the disattenuated variant (Figure 3, Appendix C) corrects for it on the benchmarks with per-rater scores.

3.6Fingerprints are stable across benchmarks

For 14 of 17 aligned metrics (82%) the SD of βS across benchmarks is at most 0.15 (pre-registered bar: 70%); the median SD falls from 0.225 for the raw correlation to 0.138 for the fingerprint.

The three exceptions (SARI, iBLEU and BERTScore against the source) are all similarity metrics; among reference-free metrics, 11 of 12 fingerprints are stable. Stability matters in practice because it makes metric rankings agree across benchmarks.

The stronger, pre-registered claim, that fingerprints predict a metric's correlations on an unseen benchmark 40% better than the naive baseline (the mean raw correlations of the other benchmarks), does not hold. The leave-one-benchmark-out error falls by 24% (0.153 vs 0.200), and the fingerprint wins for 19 of 19 metrics. The gain is concentrated where it should be: on benchmarks whose ρ is far from the others'.

4Length is part of the confound

Simplifying usually shortens. If human simplicity ratings follow length, a metric can look like a simplicity metric by rewarding brevity. How strongly simplicity and length are linked is itself a benchmark property, ranging from |r| = 0.05 on ASSET to 0.71 on Structural Simplicity. We take length as the compression ratio (output tokens / source tokens) and ask, for every metric, how much of its simplicity correlation survives once length is held fixed.

Compression ratio and sentence-length gain lose their whole simplicity signal once length is controlled and sit at chance on equal-length pairs. FKGL, FRE and ΔSLE keep 70–80% of theirs, and SIMPLE keeps 93–96%.

The pre-registered expectation was stronger: that proxies as a group lose ≥ 60% (they lose 33%) and fall to chance on matched pairs (the readability formulas reach 0.57–0.60). Readability formulas therefore carry simplicity signal beyond length, and length contaminates learned metrics far less than expected. What natural data cannot do is settle the question for the benchmark where it matters most: only on Structural Simplicity are simplicity and length strongly coupled (|r| = 0.71), and there a three-way fingerprint on (M, S, L) is precise, so the inseparability hypothesis H4 fails. Separating simplicity from length cleanly requires data in which they are uncorrelated by design.

5A factorial contrast set

We build outputs that land in the cells of a 2 × 3 × 3 design: meaning kept or lost × simpler, same or harder than the source × shorter, same length or longer. With equal cell counts the three factors are uncorrelated, so a metric's weights on meaning, simplicity and length are separately identifiable. Sources are hard-to-read sentences (20–45 tokens, FKGL ≥ 10) from 2025–26 Wikipedia, Wikinews, MedlinePlus and gov.uk, excluding near-duplicates of benchmark and metric-training data. Two open models (Gemma 3 27B, Mistral Small 3.2 24B) generate; a human validator decides whether each output realises its cell; three trained annotators rate meaning (1–5), simplicity relative to the source (−2…+2) and fluency (1–5). No metric or LLM ever selects items, since the set is meant to test exactly those scorers.

Exploratory: pilot, one annotator
The pilot has 20 sources and 199 rated items (194 designed items plus 5 attention checks), all rated by one trained annotator. Agreement (Krippendorff α) needs a second and third annotator, and single-rater means are noisier than the three-rater means the full set will use. Read the numbers below as a first look at the design, not as results.

5.1Did the design survive?

Before annotation, the designed factors and the measured length are uncorrelated on the valid items (all |r| ≤ 0.04). After annotation, the human ratings mostly follow the design: the achieved correlation between human meaning and human simplicity is 0.04, and between meaning and length −0.12. Simplicity and length are still weakly linked in the ratings (r = −0.25 against a 0.20 target): longer outputs are rated a little harder even when the design keeps their difficulty fixed.

Two weak spots stand out. In the "meaning lost" cells the single annotator often still gave high meaning scores: generated losses that drop a minor detail read as harmless, and lenient raters miss them (cell validity for meaning 0.64 against a 0.85 target). And some cells are intrinsically hard to generate: lengthening a sentence by explaining it reads as simpler even when the model is told to keep the difficulty, itself a sign of how entangled simplicity and length are.

5.2Metrics on the contrast set

The contrast set changes what the metrics appear to measure. Figure 11 sets each metric's correlations on the contrast set against its average on the natural benchmarks.

Readability formulas and learned simplicity metrics correlate 0.62–0.74 with human simplicity, far above their natural-data average, with essentially no meaning signal. Only MeaningBERT (0.51) and BERTScore against the source (0.37) track meaning; BLEU against the source, often used as a meaning proxy, does not (0.10).

Both shifts are what the confound predicts. On natural benchmarks a readability formula's simplicity correlation is diluted wherever ρ works against it, and similarity to the source looks like a meaning measure partly because faithful outputs are also the ones that copy the source. With meaning and simplicity uncorrelated by design (ρ = 0.09 in these ratings), neither distortion is there. Length ratios behave as designed against the designed simplicity labels (|r| ≤ 0.04) but keep |r| ≈ 0.25 against this annotator's ratings, the size of the residual simplicity–length link in the ratings themselves. All three pre-registered H6 predictions fail in this pilot, in an informative direction: readability formulas do not collapse (consistent with §4), and the learned metrics' fingerprints move away from their natural-data values. Both need confirming on the full set.

6LLM judges

We prompt two open-weight LLMs, Qwen3.8-27B (dense, answers directly) and gpt-oss-120b (mixture of experts, reasons before answering), with the annotators' own definitions and scales, one prompt per dimension, temperature 0, and take the expected rating over the digit probabilities (G-Eval). Judges come from families other than the generators. The natural sample is at most 2,000 items per benchmark, drawn by whole sources (5,994 items).

Status

6.1How do judges compare with the metrics?

Figure 12 ranks each judge among all non-judge metrics on each benchmark and on the contrast set. A metric enters in its useful direction (e.g. "−BLEU (src)" for simplicity), and the best metric is chosen per benchmark after the fact, which favours the metrics.

For meaning, both judges are the best or near-best measure: Qwen3.8-27B is first or tied with the best metric on 8 of 9 benchmarks and significantly better on 5; gpt-oss-120b on 7 and 3. On the contrast set both are far ahead (0.77 and 0.70 against MeaningBERT's 0.51). For simplicity, neither judge beats the best metric on any natural benchmark: both are within noise on most and below it on QATS 2016 and Structural Simplicity (gpt-oss also on Wu & Arase). On the contrast set both come first, by a margin that is significant only for Qwen (0.78 and 0.76 against SIMPLE-large's 0.74).

Two readings follow. The judges solve the meaning side, where the contrast set shows metrics to be weakest. On the simplicity side, a judge is roughly as good as the best metric, but which metric is best changes from benchmark to benchmark (LENS, SIMPLE, BLEU against the source, compression ratio), while the judges are near the top everywhere: averaged over the nine benchmarks they score 0.59 (Qwen) and 0.57 (gpt-oss) against 0.56 for the best single metric. That holds for the prompt we wrote; §6.4 shows how much it depends on it.

6.2Is the larger judge better?

No. gpt-oss-120b has four times the parameters of Qwen3.8-27B, but on meaning it is significantly lower on six of the ten data sets (paired cluster bootstrap; mean over the nine benchmarks 0.71 against 0.77), and on simplicity the two cannot be told apart anywhere except QATS 2016, where gpt-oss is lower. Figure 13 shows one reason. Qwen answers directly, so its probabilities over the five digits give a graded score, with hundreds of distinct values per benchmark. gpt-oss reasons first and then commits to one digit with probability ≈ 1, so its score takes only five to eight values on most benchmarks. Scored by its answered digit alone, Qwen drops from 0.59 to 0.51 on simplicity, below gpt-oss (0.57), and from 0.77 to 0.74 on meaning.

A comparison of LLM judges compares a model and its scoring rule together. With its graded score, the smaller judge leads on meaning and ties on simplicity; scored by digits, as a reasoning judge has to be, it still leads on meaning but trails on simplicity.

The judges also agree with each other on what they measure: their fingerprints on the natural benchmarks are almost identical (simplicity prompt βM −0.02, βS 0.61 for both; meaning prompt βM 0.81 and 0.76; Figure 6).

6.3Do judges reward shorter outputs?

We test whether a judge's simplicity score depends on length beyond what the human ratings explain: partial r(judge S, L | human M, human S). For comparison we run the same test on two metrics. On the contrast set we can also control for the design instead of the ratings (designed meaning and simplicity), which lets us run the test on the human annotator too.

The pre-registered bias finding (|partial r| ≥ 0.15 with the CI excluding 0, for at least one judge) holds on natural data for both judges: Qwen3.8-27B on three benchmarks (Wu & Arase −0.52, QATS 2016 −0.44, Scialom (human) −0.30), gpt-oss-120b on six (Wu & Arase −0.47, Scialom (human) −0.40, QATS 2016 −0.35, SimpDA 2022 −0.28, Simplicity-DA −0.20, SimpLikert 2022 −0.20). On the contrast-set pilot it is −0.38 and −0.50.

This is not the familiar verbosity bias of pairwise preference judges, which favour longer answers: for simplicity the judges lean the other way. On the natural benchmarks the judges lean on length more than a readability formula does (Wu & Arase: −0.52 and −0.47 against −0.18 for FKGL). A caveat applies: noisy human ratings under-control for true simplicity, so part of a natural-data partial correlation can be real simplicity. The contrast set gives the cleaner test, because length is uncorrelated with the design, and there the picture is broader (Figure 14, top row). Controlling for the designed factors instead of the ratings, every simplicity measure treats shorter as simpler more than the human annotator does (−0.32): gpt-oss most (−0.66), then Qwen (−0.61), but FKGL (−0.57) and SIMPLE-large (−0.54) nearly as much. The shortcut is not specific to LLM judges; they are simply not free of it.

6.4Is a judge's score a property of the model or of its prompt?

Everything above uses one prompt per dimension (j1), written with the annotators' definitions. To see how much the results depend on that wording, both judges answered the same two questions under four more prompts that ask for the judgment in very different ways (exploratory, not pre-registered):

PromptHow it asks
j1The annotators' definitions and scale; for simplicity, "think of the words and the sentence structure; this is not about length".
bareThe question and the ends of the scale only: "How much simpler is the simplified text than the original? (1 = much more complex, 5 = much simpler)". No definitions.
reversedFramed as a problem to rate, with the scale flipped: how much information is lost, changed or invented; how much harder to read the rewrite is.
readerA teacher choosing texts for adults learning English. Simplicity is rated for the rewrite on its own ("how easy would it be for one of these readers?"), not against the original.
explainThe judge first notes the changes in at most three lines, then ends with "Rating: N".

Every prompt is answered with one digit from 1 to 5, scored as before (expected rating over the digits) and mapped onto j1's scales, so only the wording changes. The items are the 199 contrast-pilot items and 60 items per natural benchmark spread over its sources (739 items, each answered under all five prompts by both judges, about 5,900 calls per judge).

For meaning the prompt hardly matters. Averaged over the nine benchmarks the five prompts give 0.72–0.79 (Qwen3.8-27B) and 0.64–0.71 (gpt-oss-120b), and the judges' standing against the metrics does not change: Qwen is significantly better than the best metric on four to six of the ten data sets under every prompt, and worse on none. For simplicity the prompt matters a great deal. j1 is the best of the five for both judges (0.60 and 0.57); no other prompt beats it significantly on any data set, and 11 (gpt-oss) and 12 (Qwen) of the 40 prompt × data-set comparisons are significantly worse. Without a definition (bare), simplicity validity falls to 0.27 and 0.26 on average, and to between −0.02 and 0.22 on ASSET and Scialom (system), where j1 reaches 0.73–0.76. With j1 the judges tie the best metric on every data set (Qwen beats it on the contrast pilot); under the other prompts they fall below it on one to seven of the ten.

A judge under two prompts agrees with itself on meaning (0.91 Qwen, 0.81 gpt-oss) but much less on simplicity (0.56 and 0.48, and 0.66 and 0.61 with bare left out). The two judges given the same j1 prompt agree on simplicity at 0.74: rewording the prompt moves a judge about as much as replacing the model. What moves is what the simplicity score measures. On the natural benchmarks its fingerprint weight on human simplicity is 0.59 and 0.57 under j1, and 0.31–0.56 under the other prompts. With explain it also picks up meaning (βM 0.23 and 0.20, against 0.03 and 0.04 under j1), and gpt-oss's bare score rewards losing meaning (βM −0.28). The meaning score's fingerprint stays where it was (βM 0.67–0.80 under every prompt). On the contrast pilot, against the design, j1's simplicity score follows designed simplicity with almost no meaning in it (βS 0.77 and 0.67, βM 0.07 and 0.04); bare weakens it (0.56 and 0.51), and the reader framing makes Qwen's meaning score partly a simplicity score (βS 0.26).

The length bias of §6.3 is not a product of j1's wording. On the contrast pilot, controlling for the design, every prompt treats shorter as simpler, for both judges (partial r −0.47 to −0.74). On the natural benchmarks the bare prompt, which lacks j1's note that simplicity "is not about length", makes the bias much stronger (Qwen: −0.25 to −0.78, meeting the pre-registered bias criterion on eight of the nine benchmarks); whether the note itself is what helps would need a prompt that differs from j1 by the note alone.

A judge's meaning score is a property of the model; its simplicity score is a property of the model and its prompt. The simplicity results of §6.1 belong to one carefully worded prompt, the best of the five we tried, and should be read as a best case. A report of LLM-judge simplicity should give the prompt and the definition of simplicity it uses, and preferably results under more than one prompt.

Caveats. The ablation is exploratory. With 60 items per natural benchmark the intervals are wide, and the 40 comparisons per judge and dimension are not corrected for multiplicity. Two prompts change more than the wording: bare drops the definition, and reader asks about the rewrite's own simplicity rather than its gain over the original. The conclusions above hold with bare left out, but a paraphrase of j1 with the same content would separate wording from definition.

7Discussion

What to do in practice. A metric's correlation with simplicity on one benchmark says as much about that benchmark's annotators as about the metric. Report the benchmark's ρ next to any correlation, report fingerprints rather than raw correlations, and choose metrics by fingerprint: their rankings agree across benchmarks (§3). For meaning, open LLM judges are now the strongest automatic measures we tested, and a 27B judge is at least as good as a 120B one; for simplicity, no single measure wins everywhere, and a judge's length bias should be checked before it is trusted. When judges are compared, they should be scored the same way (§6.2). A judge's simplicity score depends on its prompt (§6.4), so the prompt and its definition of simplicity belong in the report.

What the contrast set adds. On natural data, a metric's meaning signal and its copying of the source come together. When meaning, simplicity and length are crossed by design, most metrics that looked like meaning metrics are not, and the metrics that look like simplicity metrics mostly are. The contrast set is the evidence natural benchmarks cannot provide.

Limitations. English only. Nine benchmarks of very uneven size. Length measured as compression ratio. The contrast-set results here come from one annotator. The judges are open models from two families; closed models may behave differently, and their simplicity results depend on the prompt (§6.4). The fingerprint is linear and item-level; system-level conclusions may differ.

8Road to ACL: proposals

These are suggestions for maximising the paper's chances at ARR, January 2027. None is adopted yet; each says what it costs.

Proposal · framing
  • Lead with the confound and a recommendation, not with the method. Figure 2 (raw correlations flip, fingerprints do not) as the first figure; the take-home is "report ρ and fingerprints". The pre-registered H2 failure becomes a nuance ("transfer helps where benchmarks differ"), stated plainly; pre-registration, with its hash and deviation log, is a selling point. Cost: writing only.
  • Answer Shayegh et al. (2025) head-on in the related work: MT versus simplification, system-level pairwise accuracy versus item-level weights, no reliability correction, no transfer test. It is the paper reviewers are most likely to cite against novelty. Cost: writing only.
Proposal · analyses that pre-empt reviewer questions
  • Human ceiling for the judges. With three annotators, correlate each annotator with the mean of the others and put the judge against that ceiling. "0.77" means something different at a ceiling of 0.80 than at 0.95. Cost: one analysis once annotation is done.
  • Rater-free contrast analyses as co-primary. Every contrast-set result also against the validated design labels (already implemented). It protects the contrast-set story against annotator noise and the annotation budget. Cost: none; reporting only.
  • Score every judge the same way. A reasoning judge gives one digit; sample it several times (e.g. five samples at temperature 0.7) and average, so its score is graded like a direct judge's, and report both judges with both scoring rules. It removes the resolution confound from the judge comparison (§6.2) before a reviewer finds it. Cost: about 5× the gpt-oss calls on the contrast pilot and one natural benchmark, ~1 GPU-day on the shared machine.
  • Prompt robustness as a confirmed result (started: §6.4). Run items 61–300 of each benchmark (new items: the sampling order is fixed) with predictions written down before the run, and add two controlled prompts: j1 reworded with the same content, and j1 without its length note. The first separates wording from definition, which answers the obvious objection to the bare prompt; the second shows what the length note does. Cost: about 18 h (Qwen) and 16 h (gpt-oss) of judge time on the shared machine, two to three days.
  • A length-controlled judge. j1 already says that simplicity "is not about length", and the bias is there anyway (§6.3), and stronger without that note (§6.4). The j1-without-the-note prompt above measures what the note does; a stronger instruction, with its fingerprint and partial r, would turn the bias finding into a usable recommendation. Cost: about 3 GPU-hours on the contrast pilot.
  • Make the length test comparative (started: Figure 14, top row). Given the design, FKGL and SIMPLE lean on length almost as much as the judges, and all of them more than the annotator. Report the length test for every simplicity measure on the full set; "every automatic simplicity measure takes the length shortcut, humans less so" is a stronger and more general finding than a judge-only bias. Cost: the code exists; reporting only.
  • One closed API judge (allowed by the plan if budget permits) for external validity on the contrast set and a 2,000-item natural subsample. Cost: small; about 10k calls.
  • System-level fingerprints in an appendix, for the benchmarks with at least six systems, because many metric papers report system-level correlations. Cost: one analysis on existing scores.
Proposal · resources
  • Release the contrast set (items, cell labels, validator decisions, three-annotator ratings) with a datasheet, and a small fingerprint package that takes metric scores and human ratings and returns ρ, fingerprints, the feasibility region and matched-pair accuracy. Reusable artefacts weigh heavily in ARR reviews. Cost: about two days.
  • Fingerprint cards: one row per metric and judge, (βM, βS, R, stability, length retention, contrast-set r), as the paper's practical table. Cost: assembled from existing results.

C. Methods in brief

Statistics. Item-level Pearson correlations; 95% percentile cluster bootstrap over source sentences (B = 10,000, seed 13; B = 2,000 for the judge-versus-metric differences); DerSimonian–Laird random-effects meta-analysis on Fisher z; Holm correction within the confirmatory family {H1, H2, H3, H8}. Reliability. Split-half per dimension (random rater halves, Spearman–Brown, 200 splits); ρ* = ρ / √(RMRS) and c* = c / √R. Ranking consistency. Kendall τb between the metric rankings of two benchmarks, by raw rS and by βS. Length. L = compression ratio; partial r(X, S | L); matched pairs = two outputs of one source with |ΔL| ≤ 0.05 and clearly different human simplicity. Judges. Qwen3.8-27B (GGUF Q8_0, thinking off) and gpt-oss-120b (native MXFP4, low reasoning effort), served locally with llama.cpp; expected score over the top-10 digit log-probabilities (gpt-oss reasons before answering and puts probability ≈ 1 on one digit, so its expected score is its answered digit).

D. Related work and novelty (scan of 17 Nov 2026)

Correlated criteria in meta-evaluation. Ernst et al. (2023) show on SummEval that metrics designed for one criterion appear to correlate with others because the human criteria correlate, and remove the effect by stratifying. Shayegh et al. (2025) study the adequacy–fluency trade-off in MT and find WMT meta-evaluation favours adequacy metrics partly because of its systems. Xiao et al. (2023) bring measurement theory to metric evaluation; Pagnoni et al. (2021) use partial correlations for factuality metrics. Instability of metric conclusions across datasets is documented by Bhandari et al. (2020) and Dai et al. (2024), without an explanation through the human correlation. Psychometrics. The feasibility bound on a correlation given two others is classical (Stanley & Wang 1969; Glass & Collins 1970; Olkin 1981); correction for attenuation goes back to Spearman (1904). Simplification. SARI (Xu et al. 2016); the BLEU critique of Sulem et al. (2018); Alva-Manchego et al. (2021); SLE (Cripwell et al. 2023) observes that metrics conflate simplicity with meaning and reports different simplicity–adequacy correlations on two benchmarks; LENS, SALSA; LLM-era evaluation (Wu & Arase). Length. Napoles et al. (2011) ask for comparisons at equal compression; Martin et al. (2018) find simplicity on QATS best predicted by length features; verbosity bias of LLM judges is established in pairwise preference (Zheng et al. 2023; Dubois et al. 2024). Contrast sets. Gardner et al. (2020), CheckList, perturbation suites for metrics (DEMETR; He et al. 2023), APPLS (Guo et al. 2024): one factor at a time, mostly not human-rated.

What is new. Others observed the effect within single datasets; none models it, corrects it for rating noise, tests its predictions across benchmarks, or studies it in simplification, where the tension between meaning and simplicity defines the task. New here: the meta-analysis of ρ, reliability-corrected fingerprints with transfer and ranking tests, length deconfounding across nine benchmarks, a factorial human-rated contrast set, and the LLM-judge length test for simplicity.
